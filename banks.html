The Banks

Some of Enron's hidden borrowing ran through large banks. Senate investigators, the SEC, and the bankruptcy examiner each described how deals that looked like trading or sales worked like loans. This page sets out what each of them said, and what is still unknown.

In November 2001, Enron told its bankers that it had $25.116 billion of "debt" that did not appear as debt on its balance sheet (Chapter 5). One category on Enron's own list was $4.822 billion of "Commodity Transactions with Financial Institutions."source The sources use different standards: Senate staff "found"; the SEC "alleged" or settled without admission; and the bankruptcy examiner, Neal Batson, concluded only what a fact-finder could conclude. None of these is a court verdict.

How a prepay worked

In an ordinary prepay, a buyer pays now for something delivered later. Robert Roach, the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations' chief investigator, testified under oath that Enron's prepays were different: "when all the bells and whistles are stripped away, the basic transaction fails as a prepay, and what remains is a loan to Enron using an investment bank and an obligation on Enron's part to repay the principal plus interest."source

Roach explained why the deals had three parties. Two offsetting trades between Enron and one bank would be obvious to auditors, so, he said, Chase "inserted into the trade one of its shell corporations, Mahonia." In what he called a simplified version, Chase sent cash to Mahonia, Mahonia paid Enron, and Enron's later "deliveries" flowed back to Chase with interest built in.source (Chase disputed that it controlled Mahonia; see below.)

Why did the label matter? Booked as trading, the cash counted as money from Enron's business, not borrowing. Roach listed four tests for trading treatment: independent parties, trades not linked to each other, real price risk, and a business reason. "The Enron-type prepays we examined failed on all accounts," he testified.source He also testified that Enron treated the same deals as loans on its tax returns, so it could deduct the interest.source

Roach put the total at more than $8 billion over about six years, including $3.7 billion from 12 deals with Chase and $4.8 billion from 14 with Citigroup.source The examiner's figure for Citigroup is $4.7 billion.source By the staff's estimate, if the roughly $4 billion outstanding at the end of 2000 had been booked as debt, Enron's total debt would have risen about 40 percent, to $14 billion, and its funds flow from operations would have fallen almost 50 percent.source

Senator Carl Levin read aloud a 1998 internal Chase e-mail: "Enron loves these deals as they are able to hide funded debt from their equity analysts."source A Chase managing director testified: "I think it is an unfortunate statement."source Moody's chief credit officer testified that Moody's did not know of the prepays before the bankruptcy.source The SEC later alleged that the prepays "were in substance loans because their structure eliminated all commodity price risk that would normally exist in commodity trades."source

Four deals, 2000–2001

In January 2003 the Senate subcommittee staff reported on four deals tied to Enron's pulp-and-paper trading business. They found that "Citigroup and Chase actively aided Enron in executing them, despite knowing the transactions utilized deceptive accounting or tax strategies, in return for substantial fees or favorable consideration in other business dealings."source

Fishtail (December 2000). Enron put the trading business into a joint venture valued at $200 million. The staff found that Chase's $42 million share was a commitment that was never funded, yet Chase was paid $500,000 "for pretending to provide the bulk of financing."source

Bacchus (December 2000). Enron "sold" its Fishtail interest for $200 million to the Caymus Trust, which was funded by a $194 million Citigroup loan and $6 million of "equity" from FleetBoston whose risk was passed back to Citigroup.source Six million dollars is the 3 percent outside stake the SPE rules required (the 3 percent rule).source The staff found that Andrew Fastow gave a Citigroup banker an oral promise to protect that equity; a Citigroup credit report listed its support as "VERBAL GUARANTEES," 100 percent. The banker denied that Enron had guaranteed it, but confirmed seeking Enron's "verbal support."source The "sale" let Enron report a $112 million gain, more than 11 percent of its 2000 net income.source

Sundance (June 2001). A Citigroup committee wrote that its investment "has been structured to act like debt in form and substance."source Citigroup ended the venture on or about November 30, 2001, two days before Enron's bankruptcy, recovering its $28.5 million plus a return.source The staff found that Sundance was used to return Citigroup's $200 million from Bacchus six months later.source

Slapshot (June 2001). The staff found that Chase "cloaked a legitimate $375 million loan to Enron issued by a consortium of banks inside a $1.4 billion sham loan," using a $1 billion same-day overdraft, so that an Enron affiliate could claim Canadian tax deductions.source

Bank by bank

JPMorgan Chase. Who controlled Mahonia is disputed. Chase's witness Jeffrey Dellapina testified that "Mahonia is beneficially owned by a charitable trust. Neither Chase nor Enron has any ownership interest in Mahonia."source Senator Levin read from a 1986 letter by Chase's law firm in Jersey saying it was important that such vehicles be controlled by Chase; the passage he read named a different vehicle, and the Chase witnesses said they had never seen the letter.source The bankruptcy examiner called Mahonia and Delta "shell entities set up by JPMorgan Chase and Citigroup."source The library does not settle the question.

Citigroup. Its risk-management head, David Bushnell, testified that Citigroup relied heavily on assurances that Arthur Andersen had reviewed the prepays: "While we regret our relationship with Enron, we acted in good faith at all times."source

Merrill Lynch. The SEC alleged that in December 1999 Merrill bought an interest in Nigerian barges owned by Enron after, according to an internal Merrill document the SEC quoted, Enron had "assured us that we will be taken out of our investment within six months."source The SEC also alleged that Fastow told Merrill's bankers the guarantee could not be put in writing, because that would stop Enron from recognizing a gain on the sale.source The promised return differs by source: the SEC's complaint says 22.5 percent a year (or $250,000 plus 15 percent),source while the Senate staff report says a guaranteed 15 percent.source The examiner reported that one Merrill Lynch employee's notes called the deal a "reputational risk, i.e., aid/abet Enron income stmt. manipulation."source Two Merrill bankers, Robert Furst and Schuyler Tilney, had met voluntarily with the Senate staff. Sworn at a July 2002 hearing, they declined to answer questions after learning that one of the deals was under Justice Department investigation, invoking their Fifth Amendment right.source Invoking that right is not evidence of guilt.

CIBC. The SEC's Enron index lists a December 2003 action charging Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce and three executives with aiding and abetting Enron's fraud; CIBC settled at once, consenting to an injunction and paying $80 million.source The library holds only that listing, not the complaint.

RBS, CSFB, and Toronto Dominion. The examiner covered these three banks in appendices to his Final Report. He first explained his test: a bank would need actual knowledge of an officer's wrongdoing, and "'should have known' or 'suspicion' will not suffice."source In Appendix E he concluded that a fact-finder could conclude that the Royal Bank of Scotland aided and abetted certain Enron officers, and that its claims of about $537 million could be equitably subordinated, pushed behind other creditors'.source In Appendix F he reached the same kind of conclusion about Credit Suisse First Boston (claims of at least $417 million),source where one employee had asked whether it was acceptable to enter "such an 'obvious' loan transaction."source In Appendix G he did so for Toronto Dominion (about $57.8 million),source whose six prepays, he found, let Enron improperly record about $1.5 billion of operating cash flow.source He noted that the banks had possible defenses.source

How it ended, as far as the library shows

On July 28, 2003, JPMorgan Chase agreed to pay $135 million and Citigroup $120 million ($101 million of it for Enron dealings) to settle SEC charges, without admitting or denying them.source Citigroup's case was an order with SEC findings on its prepays and two other deals, including Bacchus.source The SEC said it would send $236 million to Enron fraud victims under the new Sarbanes-Oxley "Fair Fund" provision. The same day, both banks settled with the Manhattan District Attorney and signed agreements with bank regulators.source The terms of the District Attorney settlements are not in the library.

The SEC charged Merrill Lynch and four executives in March 2003.source How that case ended is not in the library.source Separately, the government tried four unnamed Merrill employees over the barge deal; the Fifth Circuit reversed their convictions in 2006.source The library does not name them. CIBC settled with the SEC for $80 million.source

Enron's bankruptcy estate sued several banks in September 2003, seeking more than $3 billion.source The library does not show how that suit ended. It shows no SEC or criminal action against RBS, CSFB, or Toronto Dominion; the examiner noted that Toronto Dominion was not a party to the SEC's Citigroup proceeding.source The examiner did note that one RBS banker was indicted in 2002 on allegations about a separate LJM1-related sale; the outcome is not in the library.source Absence from the library does not prove that no action was taken.

The Senate staff recommended that the SEC take enforcement action against financial institutions that help companies deceive investors.source Whether regulators adopted that recommendation is not in the library.
