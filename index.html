Ask Why: The Rise and Fall of Enron

This site tells the story of Enron in seven short chapters, written for students from any major. It is built like an annotated edition of a difficult book: every fact carries a note that points to the original document behind it, so you can check it yourself.

The name is the method. At each step, ask why: why a number was reported the way it was, why a deal was set up that way, why the people and firms whose job was to check the numbers acted as they did. Each chapter ends with an “Ask Why” question to think about or discuss.

The Story

  1. Chapter 1

    Origins

    How an Omaha pipeline company and a Houston gas company combined in 1985, and how the company they formed began to remake itself.

  2. Chapter 2

    The Business Model and Mark-to-Market Accounting

    Enron turned itself from a pipeline company into a trader, and it used an accounting method that let it report the expected profit from a deal as soon as the deal was signed.

  3. Chapter 3

    The Special Purpose Entities

    Enron used separate entities to move debt and risk off its books. Several of the most important were run by its own finance executives.

  4. Chapter 4

    Warning Signs and the Whistleblower

    Well before the collapse, people inside Enron and its audit firm raised doubts. In August 2001, one employee took hers to the chairman.

  5. Chapter 5

    The Collapse

    Between mid-October and early December 2001, a quarterly loss, a correction of past results, and a failed merger ended confidence in Enron, and the company filed for bankruptcy.

  6. Chapter 6

    Arthur Andersen

    Enron's auditor approved its books for years, then destroyed documents as investigators arrived. Its criminal conviction was later reversed, but by then the firm had collapsed.

  7. Chapter 7

    Aftermath and Reform

    Investigations, trials, lost retirement savings, and a new federal law that changed the rules for public companies and their auditors.

How to read this site

  • Source notes. Small numbers in the text, like [1], point to the document behind each fact. On a wide screen the note sits in the margin beside the text; on a phone, tap the number to open it. Each note links to the document itself, at the right page where possible. A full list, “Sources for this page,” is at the end of every chapter.
  • Glossary. Terms with a dotted underline, like mark-to-market accounting, open a short definition. The Glossary has the longer explanation.
  • Presentation mode. The Presentation button at the top makes the text larger for a classroom screen. The Dark/Light button changes the colors. The site remembers both choices.
  • Keyboard. In a chapter, the ← and → keys go to the previous or next chapter. On The Footnote, j and k step through the notes, and Esc closes an open note.
  • Careful wording. The site says who made each statement: a company filing reported, a complaint alleged, a witness testified, a court held. Being named or accused is not the same as being found guilty, and the Cast of Characters gives each person’s actual legal outcome.
