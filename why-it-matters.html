Why This Matters to You

What the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 changed for companies, executives, and auditors, the Enron problems lawmakers pointed to, and what the rules could mean for anyone starting a career in business or accounting.

President Bush signed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act on July 30, 2002.source The Congressional Research Service wrote that the law "had its genesis early in 2002 after the declared bankruptcy of the Enron Corporation," that its momentum then seemed to slow, and that the WorldCom scandal in late June renewed Congress's interest.source The Senate Banking Committee published its hearings under the title "Accounting Reform and Investor Protection Issues Raised by Enron and Other Public Companies."source

Each section below gives what the law requires, the Enron problem that lawmakers and witnesses pointed to ("said" marks a speech or statement, not a finding), and what the rule could mean for you. The final conference report explained little about individual sections,source so the Senate committee's report and the floor debates are the best guides to what Congress meant.

CEO and CFO certification (Sections 302 and 906)

What the law requires

Section 302 requires the SEC to make a company's chief executive and chief financial officer certify every annual and quarterly report. They must state that they reviewed it, that it contains no material untrue statements or omissions, that the financial statements fairly present the company's condition, and that they are responsible for its internal controls.source They must also say that they evaluated those controls within the last 90 days, and that they told the auditors and the audit committee about significant weaknesses and about "any fraud, whether or not material, that involves management."source Section 906 adds a second, criminal certification.source The Congressional Research Service summarized its penalties: up to $1 million and 10 years in prison for a knowingly false certification, and up to $5 million and 20 years for a willful one.source

The Enron problem

On November 8, 2001, Enron announced that its financial statements for 1997 through 2000, and the audit reports on them, "should not be relied upon" (see Chapter 5).source The special committee of Enron's board later found that the company's disclosures about the partnerships were "obtuse."source It also found that Lay, as chief executive, had "the ultimate responsibility" for making sure the officers reporting to him carried out their oversight of the partnerships.source The Senate committee's report stated that Section 302 in effect adopted a March 2002 proposal by President Bush, and quoted former SEC chairman Richard Breeden on "growing doubt about whether audited financial statements are believable."source At the signing, Bush said that chief executives and chief financial officers "must personally vouch for the truth and fairness of their companies' disclosures."source

What it means for you

If you work in a company's finance group, the numbers you prepare end up in a report that, under Section 302, two named executives must sign. Under the SEC's 2002 rule, those executives also certify that they have evaluated the company's disclosure controls and procedures within 90 days before filing.source So they may ask not only how you got the numbers but how the company checks them. The point of the rule is that the people at the top cannot treat the report as someone else's work.

Internal control reporting (Section 404)

What the law requires

Internal controls are a company's own checks: who may approve a deal, who reviews the numbers, how mistakes get caught. Section 404 requires each annual report to include management's statement that it is responsible for internal control over financial reporting, and management's assessment of whether those controls work. The company's auditor must then check that assessment and report on it.source That check, called an attestation, must follow the new oversight board's standards and "shall not be the subject of a separate engagement."source Section 103 requires the Board's auditing standards to make every audit report describe the auditor's testing of internal controls.source

The Enron problem

Enron's deals with its CFO's partnerships show why controls matter. The board approved an arrangement letting the CFO set up and run the LJM partnerships, which did business with Enron, and the Senate subcommittee found this an "unprecedented arrangement" despite "clear conflicts of interest" (see Chapter 3).source The special committee found that the two officers who were supposed to review those deals, the chief accounting officer and the chief risk officer, interpreted their oversight roles very narrowly.source The idea was not new: the Senate report quoted former Comptroller General Charles Bowsher, who said GAO had recommended an auditor-reviewed internal-control statement in 1996 and the SEC had not adopted it.source The committee stated that it did not intend the auditor's review to be "the basis for increased charges or fees."source That was a statement of intent; the library has no record of what Section 404 actually cost.

Two related rules deal with problems that were central at Enron. Section 401 requires the SEC to issue rules making companies disclose all material off-balance-sheet arrangements that could affect them.source Section 402 makes it illegal for a public company to make or arrange personal loans to its directors and executive officers, with exceptions set out in the statute, such as ordinary credit cards and consumer loans made on the same terms the public gets.source The Senate subcommittee had found that Enron's board failed to monitor Lay's use of a company-financed personal credit line, through which, the subcommittee's report says, he obtained over $77 million in cash from October 2000 to October 2001 and repaid it with Enron stock (see Chapter 7).sourcesource

What it means for you

Jobs in finance and accounting can involve carrying out or checking controls, such as an approval signature, a monthly reconciliation, or a second person checking a payment. That work can look like paperwork, but Section 404 made it part of what management reports to investors and the auditor checks. Under the SEC's 2003 rule, a single material weakness means management may not call its controls effective.source Since 2010, companies that are not accelerated filers no longer need the auditor's attestation, but management's own report is still required.source

The PCAOB (Sections 101 to 109)

What the law requires

Sarbanes-Oxley created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), a nonprofit corporation, not a government agency, to register audit firms, set auditing standards, and inspect firms.source It has five full-time members, and exactly two must be or have been certified public accountants.source They may not share in the profits of an accounting firm.source Starting 180 days after the SEC declared the Board ready, it became illegal for an unregistered firm to prepare or help prepare the audit report of a public company.source Each firm must tell the Board, client by client, how much it earned for audit and non-audit work.source The Board must inspect firms that audit more than 100 public companies every year, and smaller firms at least every three years.source It can fine and discipline firms and auditors; the heaviest penalties are reserved for intentional, knowing, or reckless conduct, or repeated negligence.source Public companies pay for it through fees based on their stock-market value, and the law directs penalty money to scholarships for accounting students, if Congress provides the funds.source

The Enron problem

Before 2002, auditors were overseen through a mix of public oversight and voluntary self-regulation by the profession. The Senate committee's report quoted Shaun O'Malley, who had chaired a panel on audit effectiveness, calling that system "extensive, Byzantine, and insufficient,"source and quoted former Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker saying that "governmental oversight, with investigation and enforcement powers, is necessary to assure discipline."source The profession's own watchdog, the Public Oversight Board, voted in January 2002 to disband.source The Senate committee's report said a number of witnesses emphasized that inspections "must no longer be left to 'peer reviews' of one accounting firm by another."source At Enron, Arthur Andersen had signed a clean audit opinion on the 2000 financial statements (see Chapter 6).source At the signing, Bush said, "For the first time, the accounting profession will be regulated by an independent board."source On April 25, 2003, the SEC and the PCAOB announced that the Board had been determined to be appropriately organized and able to carry out the law.source

What it means for you

If you become an auditor at a firm that audits public companies, your firm's work could be inspected by the Board, so your files would need to show what you did and why. In 2010 the Supreme Court left the Board in place (see the last section).source And if you are an accounting student, notice that Congress wrote you into the law: the scholarship clause is aimed at people like you, if Congress funds it.

Auditor independence (Sections 201 to 206)

What the law requires

Auditor independence means the auditor has no stake that could bend its judgment. Section 201 bans eight named non-audit services for companies the firm is auditing, plus any other service the PCAOB bans by rule. The named services include bookkeeping, designing financial systems, appraisals, internal audit work, management functions, investment banking, and legal services unrelated to the audit.source (The SEC's 2003 report to Congress counts the eight named services as "eight categories";source its 2003 rule release counts the Act's list, including the catch-all, as "nine categories."source) Other non-audit services, including tax work, need the audit committee's approval in advance, and those approvals must be disclosed to investors.source The lead audit partner and the reviewing partner must rotate off after five years.source The auditor must tell the audit committee about the company's critical accounting policies and about alternative treatments it discussed with management.source And a firm may not audit a company whose chief executive, CFO, controller, or chief accounting officer worked on that company's audit at the firm during the prior year, a cooling-off period.source

The Enron problem

The Senate committee's report stated that an auditor paid by its client already faces a conflict, and that the rapid growth of consulting had created "a second, more substantial conflict."source It cited SEC data that non-audit fees made up, on average, 73 percent of accounting firms' total fees.source Sources give different figures for Andersen's Enron fees in 2000 (see Chapter 6). The Senate staff found $52 million: $25 million for audit work and $27 million for consulting.source An Andersen partner testified that much of the so-called consulting was audit-type work.source The bankruptcy examiner reported about $54 million.source Breeden said he had not yet seen evidence that Andersen's handling of Enron was driven by its consulting work, "though this was most likely an element of the picture."source On the audit committee, the examiner concluded that a fact-finder could find Andersen failed its duty to make sure Enron's committee was informed about how significant unusual transactions were accounted for.source According to the bankruptcy examiner's report, Andersen's own policy rotated the lead partner after seven years, not five.source Breeden said that Enron had hired senior people from its audit team into senior financial positions.source

What it means for you

As an auditor, you may someday be asked to help a client in ways that would make you check your own work. The law draws some of those lines for you. Under the SEC's 2003 rules, the lead and reviewing partners rotate off after five years and then stay off that client for five,source other audit partners rotate after seven years with a two-year break,source and, except at very small firms, an audit partner may not be paid for selling the client non-audit services.source Under the 2002 law, if someone from the audit firm who worked on a company's audit becomes that company's CEO, CFO, controller or chief accounting officer, the firm may not audit the company again until more than a year has passed since that person last worked on its audit.

Document destruction, whistleblowers, and criminal penalties (Sections 802, 806, 807, 1102, 1107)

What the law requires

Section 802 makes it a crime, punishable by up to 20 years, to knowingly destroy or alter records to obstruct a federal investigation or bankruptcy case, including "in contemplation" of one. It also requires auditors to keep audit work papers for five years.source Section 1102 adds another crime, up to 20 years, for corruptly altering, destroying, or hiding a record to impair its use in an official proceeding.source Section 807 creates a new crime of securities fraud with a maximum of 25 years.source On July 9, 2002, when Senate Majority Leader Tom Daschle offered an amendment for Senator Patrick Leahy, he described its securities-fraud crime as "a tough new 10-year felony."source

Section 806 protects employees of public companies who report suspected securities fraud to a federal agency, Congress, or a supervisor: the company may not fire, demote, threaten, or harass them for it.source An employee who is punished can complain to the Secretary of Labor within 90 days and can go to federal court if there is no decision within 180 days; a winner can get reinstatement, back pay, and legal fees.source Section 1107 makes it a crime, punishable by up to 10 years, to retaliate against anyone for giving truthful information about a possible federal crime to law enforcement.source

The Enron problem

Enron's auditor, Arthur Andersen, told Congress that its Enron team had disposed of a "very substantial volume" of documents and e-mails, but that so far as it could determine, no audit work papers were destroyed (see Chapter 6).source The government's indictment alleged that tons of paper relating to the Enron audit were shredded.source Andersen was convicted in June 2002,source but in 2005 the Supreme Court reversed the conviction because the jury instructions were flawed.source On the Senate floor, Daschle said Leahy's amendment created "two new criminal anti-shredding provisions," and pointed to the Andersen case as showing how hard obstruction was to prove under existing law.source

Enron vice president Sherron Watkins testified that she sent Lay an anonymous letter on August 15, 2001 (see Chapter 4).source She testified that she did not confront Skilling or Fastow because she believed it "would have been a job-terminating move."source Leahy said the Enron case "demonstrates the vulnerability of corporate whistleblowers to retaliation under current law."source Daschle said, "People like Sherron Watkins of Enron will be protected from reprisal for the first time under Federal law."source

What it means for you

If you are ever told to clean out files while an investigation seems to be coming, stop and ask. And if you see something that looks like fraud, Section 806 was written to protect employees who report it through the channels it names. A law cannot remove every risk of speaking up, but this one made it illegal for a public company to punish employees for reporting through those channels. Since 2010 a separate law has also offered SEC awards to people who report (see the last section), though auditors who learn of a problem through an audit face limits on claiming them.source

What it means for you today

The site's library now carries the story past 2003, with SEC rules and guidance, two GAO reviews, a 2010 Supreme Court decision, and the current text of parts of the law and of one PCAOB standard. In January 2003 the SEC told Congress it was not recommending further reforms to accounting and auditing, choosing instead to focus on putting the Act into effect and supporting the new Board.source Here is some of what followed.

Section 404 in practice

The SEC adopted its rules for management's internal-control report in June 2003.source Management may not conclude that its controls are effective if there are one or more material weaknesses.source Before any company had complied, the SEC estimated the yearly cost of management's report at about $1.24 billion in total, a figure that left out the auditor's attestation.source The rule first set compliance for larger companies' fiscal years ending on or after June 15, 2004.source In February 2004 the SEC moved the dates to November 15, 2004 for larger companies, called accelerated filers, and to July 15, 2005 for the rest.source By spring 2006, GAO reported, the SEC had extended the smaller companies' deadline several more times.source

In April 2006 GAO found general agreement among regulators, companies, auditors and investors that the Act had a positive and significant effect on investor protection. It also found that smaller public companies bore "disproportionately higher costs (as a percentage of revenues)" in complying, especially with Section 404.source An SEC advisory committee had drafted recommendations that, if adopted by the SEC, would exempt up to 70 percent of public companies from all or part of Section 404; in 2006 these were proposals, not law.source

In June 2007 the SEC issued guidance describing "a top-down, risk-based evaluation" of internal control.source On July 25, 2007 the SEC approved the PCAOB's Auditing Standard No. 5 for auditors' internal-control audits, replacing an earlier standard.source Its current version, AS 2201, says the auditor's objective is "to express an opinion on the effectiveness of the company's internal control over financial reporting,"source and that this audit "should be integrated with" the audit of the financial statements.source

In 2010 the Dodd-Frank Act added Section 404(c): the auditor's attestation "shall not apply" to a company that is neither a "large accelerated filer" nor an "accelerated filer."source Management's own report is still required. A 2012 amendment also exempted "emerging growth companies" from the attestation.source

The auditors and their overseer

In 2010 the PCAOB itself was challenged in the Supreme Court. According to the Syllabus, a summary prepared by the Court's Reporter of Decisions rather than by the Court, the case began after the Board inspected an accounting firm, released a report critical of its auditing, and opened an investigation.source In Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB, decided June 28, 2010 by a vote of 5 to 4,source the Court held that "the dual for-cause limitations on the removal of Board members contravene the Constitution's separation of powers."source The opinion said this left Board members removable by the SEC at will, and that the Board's existence does not violate the separation of powers.source In the Syllabus's words, "the Board may continue to function as before."source

In July 2003 GAO reported that, after mergers and Andersen's dissolution, four firms audited over 78 percent of U.S. public companies (see Chapter 6).source In January 2008 it found that the four largest firms still audited almost all large public companies, and that 82 percent of Fortune 1000 companies saw their choice of auditor as limited to three or fewer firms.source GAO saw no compelling need for immediate action, but warned that "the loss of another large firm would further reduce large companies' auditor choice."source

Whistleblowers

The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 also created SEC awards. When a whistleblower voluntarily gives the SEC original information that leads to a successful action with sanctions over $1 million, the SEC must pay an award of 10 to 30 percent, in total, of the money collected.source The same law protects such whistleblowers from retaliation; an employee who wins can get reinstatement and double back pay with interest.source

What the library still doesn't cover

The library still has no PCAOB inspection reports on the largest firms, no PCAOB standards other than AS 2201, and nothing on later changes beyond the documents named here, so read this section as signposts rather than a full guide to today's rules.

The idea behind the law does not depend on later rules. As an accountant, auditor, analyst, manager, or employee, you may one day be asked to sign, approve, file, or stay quiet about something that does not look right. Sarbanes-Oxley put names on signatures, outside eyes on auditors, and penalties on shredding and on punishing people who speak up. Knowing why is part of knowing your job.

Ask Why

Sarbanes-Oxley answered Enron mostly with more signatures, more reviewers, and tougher penalties. Imagine you are a new employee who notices something that looks wrong. Which of these changes, if any, would make it easier for you to speak up, and what can a law not do?
