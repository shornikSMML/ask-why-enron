Origins

How an Omaha pipeline company and a Houston gas company combined in 1985, and how the company they formed began to remake itself.

Enron's story is usually told as a story about Houston in the late 1990s. But the company began much earlier, and far from Texas. It was incorporated in 1930 as Northern Natural Gas Company. It changed its name to InterNorth in 1980, and to Enron Corp. in April 1986.source

Two pipeline companies

Natural gas travels from the wells where it is produced to the homes and factories that burn it through long underground pipelines. Owning pipelines has traditionally been a steady, closely regulated business, and both of Enron's parent companies were in it.

The larger parent was InterNorth, based in Omaha, Nebraska.source At the end of 1984 it had about 35,000 miles of natural gas pipeline, $6.1 billion in total assets, 10,551 employees, and $7.5 billion in revenue for the year. The smaller parent, Houston Natural Gas (HNG), had about 14,000 miles of pipeline, 3,100 employees, and $3.9 billion in assets.source

On July 1, 1985, InterNorth bought HNG. It paid HNG's shareholders $2.4 billion in cash for all of their stock.source (One later report, by the bankruptcy examiner, dates the deal to May 1985, citing a newspaper article. This site follows the date given by the staff of Congress's Joint Committee on Taxation.source)

Joined together, the two pipeline networks ran about 37,000 miles. The congressional tax staff described the result as the largest natural gas pipeline system in the United States and the first nationwide one.source

Kenneth Lay takes charge

HNG was the smaller company, but its managers ended up running the combined business. HNG's chairman and chief executive, Kenneth Lay, became chairman and CEO of the merged company, then called HNG InterNorth, in February 1986. By the end of that year, most of the company's officers and directors came from HNG.source

Lay would lead Enron from its formation in 1986 until early 2002. He was chief executive until February 2001, when Jeffrey Skilling took that job, and became chief executive again after Skilling left in August 2001.source Chapter 4 picks up that part of the story.

The early years involved cutting back as well as growing. Sales of businesses and layoffs reduced the workforce from 8,800 in 1985 to 7,200 at the end of 1986.source

From the start, the new company had the same outside auditor: Arthur Andersen, which had audited InterNorth and became Enron's auditor when the combined company was formed.source An auditor is an independent accounting firm that checks a company's financial statements and gives an opinion on whether they present its finances fairly. Chapter 6 tells how that relationship ended.

"From the reservoir to the burner tip"

As late as 1990, Enron still described itself in simple terms. Its 1989 annual report, as quoted by the congressional tax staff, said: "Enron's business is natural gas, from the reservoir to the burner tip."source

That was already starting to change. Enron began trading natural gas through forward contracts in 1989: agreements to buy or sell gas at a fixed price on a future date. In 1992 it adopted mark-to-market accounting for its trading operations, a method explained in Chapter 2. In 1994 it began buying and selling electricity too.source

A new executive helped drive the change. According to the Supreme Court's later account, Lay hired Jeffrey Skilling in 1990 to head an Enron subsidiary.source According to the federal indictment later filed against Skilling (a set of accusations, not findings), he had worked for the consulting firm McKinsey & Co. from 1979 to 1990, and had provided consulting services to Enron, before Enron hired him in August 1990.source

Growing fast

In 1996, Enron introduced a plan it called "Enron 2000." The plan set three financial targets: $1 billion of net income (profit after all costs and taxes) by the year 2000, 15 percent average growth each year, and double-digit growth in every single year.source Targets like these are public promises. Investors watch to see whether a company keeps them.

Enron also kept buying. In July 1997 it acquired Portland General, an Oregon electric utility with about 685,000 customers, for about $3 billion, including $1.1 billion of debt that Enron took over. Enron then reincorporated in Oregon.source

On paper, the growth was dramatic. Enron's reported revenue rose from $13 billion in 1996 to $101 billion in 2000. Its reported total assets rose from $16.1 billion to $65.5 billion over the same years.source (These are the figures Enron reported at the time. Some were later corrected, as Chapter 5 explains.) At the end of 2000, Enron said it had about 20,600 employees.source

By then, Enron described itself very differently than it had a decade before. Its 2000 annual report, again as quoted by the congressional tax staff, said Enron had "metamorphosed from an asset-based pipeline and power generating company to a marketing and logistics company whose biggest assets are its well-established business approach and its innovative people."source

Investors valued the company highly. The congressional tax staff reported that Enron's market capitalization (the total value of all its shares on the stock market) grew from about $2 billion in the mid-1980s to about $70 billion in early 2001.source

In 2001, Fortune magazine ranked Enron seventh on its list of the 500 largest U.S. companies, measured by revenue.sourcesource Fortune had also named Enron its "Most Innovative Company in America" several years running.source

That ranking is worth a second look. Revenue is the total value of what a company sells. It is not profit, and it is not what the company is worth. As Chapter 2 explains, the way Enron did its trading could make its revenue very large compared with what it actually earned.

Ask Why

In 2001, Enron ranked seventh among U.S. companies by revenue. Revenue measures how much a company sells, not how much it earns or what it owns. If you wanted to know how big or how successful a company really is, which number would you look at first, and why?
