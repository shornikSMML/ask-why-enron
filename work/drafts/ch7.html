Aftermath and Reform

Investigations, trials, lost retirement savings, and a new federal law that changed the rules for public companies and their auditors.

The investigations

Several bodies investigated what went wrong, and their conclusions carry different weight. Enron's own board formed a special committee in late October 2001, later chaired by William Powers Jr., dean of the University of Texas law school. Its lawyers reviewed more than 430,000 pages of documents and interviewed more than 65 people, but Fastow, Kopper, and Glisan declined to be interviewed on most issues, and the committee could not force anyone to testify.source Its report traced what happened to "failures at many levels and by many people," including "self-enrichment by employees," "inattentive oversight," and "a culture that appears to have encouraged pushing the limits."source Two of its three members concluded that "the Board of Directors failed, in our judgment, in its oversight duties."source Powers told a Senate committee in February 2002: "We found a systematic and pervasive attempt by Enron's management to misrepresent the company's financial condition."source

The Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations looked at the board. It found that the board "failed to safeguard Enron shareholders" and contributed to the collapse by allowing high-risk accounting, conflicts of interest, and undisclosed activity kept off the books.source Five directors, past and present, testified under oath in May 2002.sourcesource All of them rejected any share of responsibility, and the subcommittee said its investigation "did not substantiate the claims that the Enron Board members challenged management and asked tough questions."source One director, John H. Duncan (no relation to Andersen's David Duncan), testified: "We worked hard. We prepared for meetings. We asked probing questions ..."source Another, Herbert Winokur Jr., testified that he saw Enron as "a cautionary reminder of the limits of a director's role."source

The subcommittee also found that the board failed to monitor Kenneth Lay's use of a company-financed personal credit line. It reported that from October 2000 to October 2001, Lay obtained over $77 million in cash this way and repaid it only with Enron stock.source Asked about it, the chairman of the board's compensation committee, Charles LeMaistre, testified: "We had never had any responsibility to monitor this."source

Finally, in the bankruptcy case, an examiner, Neal Batson, was appointed to investigate Enron's special purpose entities.source He concluded there was sufficient evidence for a fact-finder to conclude that Lay and Skilling breached their fiduciary duties, the legal duties officers and directors owe the company, by failing to respond to red flags about the misuse of SPEs, and that certain outside directors breached their duty of good faith by approving the Rhythms and certain Raptor hedges.source He also concluded that the evidence does not support a conclusion that the outside directors acted in bad faith in failing to respond to red flags.source These are civil conclusions about what a court could find, not criminal findings. The library documents show no charges against the outside directors.source

The trials

By July 2004, the Justice Department said eleven defendants had been convicted in its Enron cases.source How each case ended matters, and in several cases the library's documents tell only part of the story.

Michael Kopper, who ran Chewco, pleaded guilty on August 21, 2002 to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and money laundering and agreed to forfeit $4 million, according to the SEC.source In October 2002 the SEC charged Andrew Fastow, Enron's former chief financial officer, alleging that he ran a scheme to defraud Enron's investors and enrich himself.source The SEC's Enron index lists a January 2004 settlement of those civil charges.source In February 2004 the Justice Department listed Fastow among the defendants "convicted to date" in its Enron cases.source The library's documents do not say what he was convicted of or what sentence he received. The former treasurer, Ben Glisan, was also listed as convicted.source Both Fastow and Glisan later testified as government witnesses at the trial of Lay and Skilling.source

Richard Causey, the former chief accounting officer, pleaded guilty on December 28, 2005, three weeks before that trial was set to begin.source The SEC later settled its civil case against him and barred him from serving as an officer or director of a public company.source

Kenneth Lay appeared before a Senate committee in February 2002, was sworn in, and then declined to answer questions, invoking his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.source Invoking that right is not evidence of guilt. In July 2004 he was indicted on charges including securities fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud.source In 2006 a jury convicted him of every count against him.source Lay died on July 5, 2006. According to the federal appeals court, his death caused the trial court to vacate his conviction and dismiss his indictment.source In law, he does not stand convicted.

Jeffrey Skilling was indicted in February 2004.source In May 2006 a jury found him guilty of nineteen counts, including conspiracy, securities fraud, and one count of insider trading, and not guilty of nine other insider-trading counts.source He was sentenced to 292 months in prison.source In 2009 a federal appeals court upheld his convictions but ordered a new sentence.source In 2010 the Supreme Court held that one legal theory used against him, "honest-services" fraud, covers only bribes and kickbacks, which the government never alleged he took. It affirmed the lower court in part, vacated it in part, and sent the case back.source The Court did not reverse his convictions outright. It left the lower court to decide whether the error was harmless and whether it affected his other convictions.source What happened after that is not covered by the library's documents.

Banks faced civil cases. In 2003, without admitting or denying the SEC's allegations, J.P. Morgan Chase agreed to pay $135 million and Citigroup $120 million to settle SEC charges. Of Citigroup's payment, $101 million related to its Enron dealings and the rest to another company, Dynegy. The charges concerned helping Enron disguise loans.source Arthur Andersen's conviction, as Chapter 6 described, was reversed in 2005.source

The employees' savings

Many Enron employees saved for retirement through a 401(k) plan. The GAO, Congress's investigative and audit agency, testified that Enron matched employees' contributions, but the match was invested only in Enron stock, and employees could not move it until age 50.source According to Labor Department figures cited by the GAO, 63 percent of the plan's assets were in company stock at the end of 2000.source

In the fall of 2001, as the stock fell, the plan went through a "lockdown," during which employees could not make changes, while it switched recordkeepers. The GAO called that an acceptable practice, but noted that executives faced no similar limits on company stock they held outside the plan.source Accounts of the lockdown's dates differ. One employee told the committee, in her written statement, that her notice said she could not access her account from October 20 to November 19, 2001.source A congressman described an "October 16 lockdown," and an employee of Enron's Oregon utility said workers there were effectively locked out in late September.sourcesource The congressman told the committee that the plan lost "about $1 billion in value."source The Labor Department opened an investigation of Enron's pension plans on November 16, 2001.source

A new law

On July 30, 2002, President Bush signed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.source At the signing he said, "The auditors will be audited."source The law's main changes include:

  • A new overseer for auditors, the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), a nonprofit corporation that registers audit firms, sets auditing standards, and inspects firms.source Exactly two of its five members must be, or have been, certified public accountants.source The SEC declared it ready to operate on April 25, 2003.source
  • Limits on what auditors may sell the companies they audit, such as bookkeeping and many kinds of consulting. Other services, including tax work, need the audit committee's advance approval.source
  • Rotation of the lead audit partner after five years,source shorter than the seven years under Andersen's own policy.source
  • Personal certification: the chief executive and chief financial officer must certify each annual and quarterly report,source and a separate certification carries criminal penalties of up to 20 years in prison if willfully false.source
  • A yearly report by management on the company's internal controls over financial reporting, which the auditor must also check.source
  • A new crime of destroying records to obstruct a federal investigation, punishable by up to 20 years, and a requirement that auditors keep audit work papers for five years.source
  • Protection for employees who report suspected fraud against shareholders or violations of SEC rules to regulators, Congress, or a supervisor.source

Ask Why

Sarbanes-Oxley requires a company's chief executive and chief financial officer to personally certify every annual and quarterly report. Can a rule like that change how people behave, or does it mainly change who is held responsible when something goes wrong?
