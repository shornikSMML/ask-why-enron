Why This Matters to You

What the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 changed for companies, executives, and auditors, the Enron problems lawmakers pointed to, and what the rules could mean for anyone starting a career in business or accounting.

President Bush signed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act on July 30, 2002.{{C-070}} The Congressional Research Service wrote that the law "had its genesis early in 2002 after the declared bankruptcy of the Enron Corporation," that its momentum then seemed to slow, and that the WorldCom scandal in late June renewed Congress's interest.{{S-077}} The Senate Banking Committee published its hearings under the title "Accounting Reform and Investor Protection Issues Raised by Enron and Other Public Companies."{{S-018}}

Each section below gives what the law requires, the Enron problem that lawmakers and witnesses pointed to ("said" marks a speech or statement, not a finding), and what the rule could mean for you. The final conference report explained little about individual sections,{{S-079}} so the Senate committee's report and the floor debates are the best guides to what Congress meant.

CEO and CFO certification (Sections 302 and 906)

What the law requires

Section 302 requires the SEC to make a company's chief executive and chief financial officer certify every annual and quarterly report. They must state that they reviewed it, that it contains no material untrue statements or omissions, that the financial statements fairly present the company's condition, and that they are responsible for its internal controls.{{C-076}} They must also say that they evaluated those controls within the last 90 days, and that they told the auditors and the audit committee about significant weaknesses and about "any fraud, whether or not material, that involves management."{{S-043}} Section 906 adds a second, criminal certification.{{S-048}} The Congressional Research Service summarized its penalties: up to $1 million and 10 years in prison for a knowingly false certification, and up to $5 million and 20 years for a willful one.{{C-077}}

The Enron problem

On November 8, 2001, Enron announced that its financial statements for 1997 through 2000, and the audit reports on them, "should not be relied upon" (see Chapter 5).{{A-083}} The special committee of Enron's board later found that the company's disclosures about the partnerships were "obtuse."{{A-064}} It also found that Lay, as chief executive, bore ultimate responsibility.{{A-062}} The Senate committee's report stated that Section 302 in effect adopted a March 2002 proposal by President Bush, and quoted former SEC chairman Richard Breeden on "growing doubt about whether audited financial statements are believable."{{S-044}} At the signing, Bush said that chief executives and chief financial officers "must personally vouch for the truth and fairness of their companies' disclosures."{{S-046}}

What it means for you

If you work in a company's finance group, the numbers you prepare end up in a report that, under Section 302, two named executives must sign. Expect them to ask how you got those numbers. The point of the rule is that the people at the top cannot treat the report as someone else's work.

Internal control reporting (Section 404)

What the law requires

Internal controls are a company's own checks: who may approve a deal, who reviews the numbers, how mistakes get caught. Section 404 requires each annual report to include management's statement that it is responsible for internal control over financial reporting, and management's assessment of whether those controls work. The company's auditor must then check that assessment and report on it.{{C-078}} That check, called an attestation, must follow the new oversight board's standards and "shall not be the subject of a separate engagement."{{S-049}} A separate section requires every audit report to describe the auditor's testing of internal controls.{{S-006}}

The Enron problem

Enron's deals with its CFO's partnerships show why controls matter. The board approved an arrangement letting the CFO set up and run the LJM partnerships, which did business with Enron, and the Senate subcommittee found this an "unprecedented arrangement" despite "clear conflicts of interest" (see Chapter 3).{{B-072}} The special committee found that the two officers who were supposed to review those deals, the chief accounting officer and the chief risk officer, interpreted their oversight roles very narrowly.{{A-062}} The idea was not new: the Senate report noted that GAO had recommended an auditor-reviewed internal-control statement in 1996, and that the SEC had not adopted it.{{S-050}} The committee stated that it did not intend the auditor's review to be "the basis for increased charges or fees."{{S-051}} That was a statement of intent; the library has no record of what Section 404 actually cost.

Two related rules deal with problems that were central at Enron. Section 401 requires companies to disclose all material off-balance-sheet arrangements that could affect them.{{S-052}} Section 402 makes it illegal for a public company to make or arrange personal loans to its directors and executive officers, with exceptions set out in the statute, such as ordinary credit cards and consumer loans made on the same terms the public gets.{{S-060}} The Senate subcommittee had found that Enron's board failed to monitor Lay's use of a company-financed personal credit line, through which, the subcommittee's report says, he obtained over $77 million in cash from October 2000 to October 2001 and repaid it with Enron stock (see Chapter 7).{{B-016}}

What it means for you

Many entry-level jobs involve carrying out, testing, or documenting controls: an approval signature, a monthly reconciliation, a second person checking a payment. It can look like paperwork, but Section 404 made it part of what management reports to investors and the auditor checks.

The PCAOB (Sections 101 to 109)

What the law requires

Sarbanes-Oxley created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), a nonprofit corporation, not a government agency, to register audit firms, set auditing standards, and inspect firms.{{C-072}} It has five full-time members, and exactly two must be or have been certified public accountants.{{C-073}} They may not share in the profits of an accounting firm.{{S-003}} Once the Board was running, it became illegal for an unregistered firm to prepare or help prepare the audit report of a public company.{{S-004}} Each firm must tell the Board, client by client, how much it earned for audit and non-audit work.{{S-005}} The Board must inspect firms that audit more than 100 public companies every year, and smaller firms at least every three years.{{S-008}} It can fine and discipline firms and auditors; the heaviest penalties are reserved for intentional, knowing, or reckless conduct, or repeated negligence.{{S-010}} Public companies pay for it through fees based on their stock-market value, and the law directs penalty money to scholarships for accounting students, if Congress provides the funds.{{S-013}}

The Enron problem

Before 2002, auditors were overseen through a mix of public oversight and voluntary self-regulation by the profession. The Senate committee's report quoted Shaun O'Malley, who had chaired a panel on audit effectiveness, calling that system "extensive, Byzantine, and insufficient,"{{S-014}} and quoted former Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker saying that "governmental oversight, with investigation and enforcement powers, is necessary to assure discipline."{{S-015}} The profession's own watchdog, the Public Oversight Board, voted in January 2002 to disband.{{S-016}} Witnesses said inspections "must no longer be left to 'peer reviews' of one accounting firm by another."{{S-017}} At Enron, Arthur Andersen had signed a clean audit opinion on the 2000 financial statements (see Chapter 6).{{A-027}} At the signing, Bush said, "For the first time, the accounting profession will be regulated by an independent board."{{S-025}} On April 25, 2003, the SEC and the PCAOB announced that the Board had been determined to be appropriately organized and able to carry out the law.{{C-081}}

What it means for you

If you become an auditor at a firm that audits public companies, the 2002 law means your firm's work can be inspected by outsiders, so your files need to show what you did and why. And if you are an accounting student, notice that Congress wrote you into the law: the scholarship clause is aimed at people like you.

Auditor independence (Sections 201 to 206)

What the law requires

Auditor independence means the auditor has no stake that could bend its judgment. Section 201 bans eight named non-audit services for companies the firm is auditing, plus any other service the PCAOB bans by rule. The named services include bookkeeping, designing financial systems, appraisals, internal audit work, management functions, investment banking, and legal services unrelated to the audit.{{C-074}} (The SEC's 2003 report counts the eight named services as "eight categories."{{S-042}}) Other non-audit services, including tax work, need the audit committee's approval in advance, and those approvals must be disclosed to investors.{{S-033}} The lead audit partner and the reviewing partner must rotate off after five years.{{C-075}} The auditor must tell the audit committee about the company's critical accounting policies and about alternative treatments it discussed with management.{{S-036}} And a firm may not audit a company whose chief executive, CFO, controller, or chief accounting officer worked on that company's audit at the firm during the prior year, a cooling-off period.{{S-037}}

The Enron problem

The Senate committee's report stated that an auditor paid by its client already faces a conflict, and that the rapid growth of consulting had created "a second, more substantial conflict."{{S-027}} It cited SEC data that non-audit fees made up 73 percent of accounting firms' total fees.{{S-026}} Sources give different figures for Andersen's Enron fees in 2000 (see Chapter 6). The Senate staff found $52 million: $25 million for audit work and $27 million for consulting.{{C-019}} Andersen's witnesses testified that much of the so-called consulting was audit-type work.{{C-020}} The bankruptcy examiner reported about $54 million.{{C-021}} Breeden said he had not yet seen evidence that Andersen's handling of Enron was driven by its consulting work, "though this was most likely an element of the picture."{{S-030}} On the audit committee, the examiner concluded that a fact-finder could find Andersen failed its duty to make sure Enron's committee was informed about how significant unusual transactions were accounted for.{{C-031}} Andersen's own policy rotated the lead partner after seven years, not five.{{C-036}} Breeden said that Enron had hired senior people from its audit team into senior financial positions.{{S-038}}

What it means for you

As an auditor, you may someday be asked to help a client in ways that would make you check your own work. The law draws some of those lines for you. If you are ever offered a senior finance job at a company you audited, the cooling-off rule applies to you.

Document destruction, whistleblowers, and criminal penalties (Sections 802, 806, 807, 1102, 1107)

What the law requires

Section 802 makes it a crime, punishable by up to 20 years, to knowingly destroy or alter records to obstruct a federal investigation or bankruptcy case, including "in contemplation" of one. It also requires auditors to keep audit work papers for five years.{{C-079}} Section 1102 adds another crime, up to 20 years, for corruptly altering, destroying, or hiding a record to impair its use in an official proceeding.{{S-067}} Section 807 creates a new crime of securities fraud with a maximum of 25 years.{{S-075}} On July 9, 2002, when Senate Majority Leader Tom Daschle offered an amendment for Senator Patrick Leahy, he described its securities-fraud crime as "a tough new 10-year felony."{{S-076}}

Section 806 protects employees of public companies who report suspected securities fraud to a federal agency, Congress, or a supervisor: the company may not fire, demote, threaten, or harass them for it.{{C-080}} An employee who is punished can complain to the Secretary of Labor within 90 days and can go to federal court if there is no decision within 180 days; a winner can get reinstatement, back pay, and legal fees.{{S-069}} Section 1107 makes it a crime, punishable by up to 10 years, to retaliate against anyone for giving truthful information about a possible federal crime to law enforcement.{{S-074}}

The Enron problem

Enron's auditor, Arthur Andersen, told Congress that its Enron team had disposed of a "very substantial volume" of documents and e-mails, but that so far as it could determine, no audit work papers were destroyed (see Chapter 6).{{C-014}} The government's indictment alleged that tons of paper relating to the Enron audit were shredded.{{G-016}} Andersen was convicted in June 2002,{{C-038}} but in 2005 the Supreme Court reversed the conviction because the jury instructions were flawed.{{G-018}} On the Senate floor, Daschle said Leahy's amendment created "two new criminal anti-shredding provisions," and pointed to the Andersen case as showing how hard obstruction was to prove under existing law.{{S-065}}

Enron vice president Sherron Watkins testified that she sent Lay an anonymous letter on August 15, 2001 (see Chapter 4).{{B-055}} She testified that she did not confront Skilling or Fastow because she believed it "would have been a job-terminating move."{{B-057}} Leahy said the Enron case "demonstrates the vulnerability of corporate whistleblowers to retaliation under current law."{{S-070}} Daschle said, "People like Sherron Watkins of Enron will be protected from reprisal for the first time under Federal law."{{S-071}}

What it means for you

If you are ever told to clean out files while an investigation seems to be coming, stop and ask. And if you see something that looks like fraud, Section 806 was written to protect employees who report it through the channels it names. A law cannot remove every risk of speaking up, but this one made punishing people for it illegal.

What it means for you today

This site's source library covers the law and its first year, 2002 and 2003. In January 2003 the SEC told Congress it was not recommending further reforms to accounting and auditing, choosing instead to focus on putting the Act into effect and supporting the new Board.{{S-020}} In July 2003 the GAO reported that, after mergers and Andersen's dissolution, four firms audited over 78 percent of U.S. public companies (see Chapter 6).{{S-023}}

This site does not yet cover what came later: how Section 404 has worked in practice and what it has cost, the PCAOB's standards and inspections, and later changes to the law. The site's owner may add official documents on these. Until then, read this page as a picture of what Congress decided in 2002, not a guide to today's rules.

The idea behind the law does not depend on later rules. As an accountant, auditor, analyst, manager, or employee, you may one day be asked to sign, approve, file, or stay quiet about something that does not look right. Sarbanes-Oxley put names on signatures, outside eyes on auditors, and penalties on shredding and on punishing people who speak up. Knowing why is part of knowing your job.

Ask Why

Sarbanes-Oxley answered Enron mostly with more signatures, more reviewers, and tougher penalties. Imagine you are a new employee who notices something that looks wrong. Which of these changes, if any, would make it easier for you to speak up, and what can a law not do?
