The Business Model and Mark-to-Market Accounting

Enron turned itself from a pipeline company into a trader, and it used an accounting method that let it report the expected profit from a deal as soon as the deal was signed.

By 2000, Enron's annual report described a company that "provides products and services related to natural gas, electricity and communications to wholesale and retail customers." It did this through pipelines, an electric utility in the Northwest, power plants, the buying and selling of energy, retail energy services, and a new broadband network.{{A-017}}

A trader, not just a pipeline

The fastest-growing part of that list was trading. In late 1999 Enron launched EnronOnline, a website where business customers could see Enron's prices and trade with it directly. Enron's description said customers could "complete commodity transactions with Enron as principal, with no direct interaction."{{A-018}}

"As principal" is an important phrase. It means Enron was not a matchmaker between other buyers and sellers. In every trade, Enron itself was the buyer or the seller. Enron reported that its trading volumes rose 59 percent in 2000, EnronOnline's first full year.{{A-018}}

Enron said it no longer needed to own so many pipelines and power plants to do this business. Its 2000 annual report stated that "Enron believes that it no longer needs to own the same level of physical assets, instead utilizing contracting and market-making activities."{{A-019}} The balance of its business shifted accordingly. From 1992 to 2000, Enron's reported risk-management assets grew from $0.5 billion to $21 billion. Over the same years, its plant and equipment grew only from $6.5 billion to $11.7 billion.{{A-009@p. 64, Part Two, II.B.3#92}}

Revenue and profit

Enron's reported revenue rose from $13.3 billion in 1996 to $40.1 billion in 1999, and then to $100.8 billion in 2000.{{A-024}} Its reported net income grew far more slowly: $703 million in 1998, $893 million in 1999, and $979 million in 2000.{{A-025}} In 2000, revenue more than doubled while profit rose by about a tenth.

Part of the explanation is how trading is counted. When a company is itself the buyer or seller in each trade, the whole sale price can be counted as revenue, even if the company keeps only a thin slice of it as profit. A trader can therefore show huge revenue and modest profit at the same time. That is one reason a ranking by revenue, like the Fortune list in Chapter 1, can say little about how much a company earns.

What mark-to-market accounting means

The accounting method at the center of Enron's trading business is called mark-to-market accounting. Enron adopted it for its trading operations in 1992.{{A-007}}

Under the traditional approach, a company records profit as it earns it: when it delivers the gas, or when the customer pays. Under mark-to-market accounting, a company values each contract at its current market value, called its fair value, at the end of every reporting period. Any change in that value counts as a gain or loss right away, even though no cash has changed hands. A gain like this is called an unrealized gain.

For something with a daily market price, such as a share of stock, this is straightforward: you look up the price. For a long-term energy contract, there may be no market price at all. Someone has to estimate future prices, costs, and interest rates, and then work out what the whole contract is worth today.

An illustration, with made-up numbers. Suppose a company signs a ten-year contract that it expects will earn $10 a year. Under the traditional approach, it reports $10 of profit each year for ten years. Under mark-to-market accounting, it estimates what all ten years of profit are worth in today's money, say about $70, and reports that amount as profit in year one. In later years it reports only the changes in its estimate. The cash the contract brings in is exactly the same either way. What changes is when the profit shows up, and how much depends on the company's own estimates. If the estimates later prove too hopeful, the company has to report losses in later years.

Enron's own accounting policy, printed in its 2000 annual report, said it valued its trading contracts at fair value and that "Unrealized gains and losses from newly originated contracts, contract restructurings and the impact of price movements are recognized as 'Other Revenues.'" Where market prices had to be estimated, the policy said they reflected management's "best estimate."{{A-020}}

Enron had checked with its regulator first. The staff of the Senate Governmental Affairs Committee found that on January 30, 1992, the Securities and Exchange Commission's Office of the Chief Accountant told Enron it would not object to mark-to-market accounting for an Enron subsidiary beginning in 1992. Enron replied that it would adopt the method from the start of 1991 instead. The staff wrote: "Apparently, the SEC did not respond further to this correspondence."{{C-055}}

Marking investments to market

Enron applied the same idea to stakes it owned in other companies, which it called merchant investments. Enron's annual report explained: "Earnings from these activities, which are accounted for on a fair value basis and are included in revenues, result from changes in the market value of the securities."{{A-021}}

This meant that when the stock of a company Enron had invested in went up, Enron's reported profit went up, and when it went down, Enron's profit went down. One such stake was in an internet company called Rhythms NetConnections. The board's special committee (the investigation described in Chapter 7) found that Enron "marked the Rhythms position to market, meaning that increases and decreases in the value of Rhythms stock were reflected on Enron's income statement."{{A-022}} Chapter 3 shows what Enron did to protect those gains.

Why the balance sheet mattered

Enron had made public promises of steady growth, including $1 billion of net income by 2000 under its "Enron 2000" plan.{{A-008}} The board's special committee found that Enron's growth required large investments up front. Borrowing more money would put its credit rating at risk, and issuing more stock would spread its earnings over more shares. The committee wrote that "Maintaining Enron's credit ratings at investment grade was vital to the conduct of its energy trading business."{{A-028}}

A credit rating is a grade that agencies such as Standard & Poor's and Moody's give to a borrower. The top grades are called investment grade. For a trader, a good rating matters a great deal: other companies will only sign long-term contracts with a trading partner they trust to pay.

The committee found that Enron's management therefore preferred to keep some assets and debts off the company's balance sheet, the statement of what a company owns and owes. This treatment, known as "off-balance-sheet", "would enable Enron to present itself more attractively as measured by the ratios favored by Wall Street analysts and rating agencies." The committee found that Enron used such arrangements in many parts of its business, including for its Houston headquarters.{{A-029}}

At the end of 2000, Enron reported total assets of $65.5 billion, debt of $10.2 billion, and shareholders' equity of $11.5 billion.{{A-026}} Arthur Andersen's audit report, dated February 23, 2001, said these financial statements "present fairly, in all material respects, the financial position of Enron Corp. and subsidiaries."{{A-027}}

Years later, the court-appointed bankruptcy examiner concluded that if Enron had not used six particular accounting techniques, it would have reported $22.1 billion of debt for 2000, not $10.2 billion.{{A-067}} The main tool for keeping debt off the books was the special purpose entity, the subject of the next chapter.

Ask Why

Mark-to-market accounting lets a company report today the profit it expects from a contract that runs for many years. When a contract has no market price, who should decide what it is worth today: the company, its auditor, or a regulator? What would each of them need in order to decide well?
