The Special Purpose Entities

Enron used separate entities to move debt and risk off its books. Several of the most important were run by its own finance executives.

A special purpose entity, or SPE, is a separate company or partnership set up for one narrow job, such as owning a single power plant or holding a pool of loans. Companies use them for many ordinary purposes. The accounting question is whether the SPE's assets and debts belong on the company's own balance sheet or can be left off.

The board's special committee, which investigated Enron after its collapse, summarized the rules of the time. A company could treat an SPE as independent only if "an owner independent of the company" made an equity investment of "at least 3% of the SPE's assets," kept that 3% "at risk throughout the transaction," and controlled the SPE.{{A-030}} Money was not "at risk" if a guarantee or similar promise protected it.{{A-030@p. 39, I. Background: Enron and Special Purpose Entities}} If the test failed, the company had to consolidate the SPE, which means adding its assets and debts to its own books.

An illustration, with made-up numbers. A company sets up an SPE to buy one of its power plants for $100 million. Banks lend the SPE $97 million, and an outside investor puts in $3 million of its own money, which it could lose. If the rules are met, the company records $100 million of cash from a sale, and the $97 million loan appears nowhere on its balance sheet. But if the "outside" $3 million was secretly guaranteed by the company, nobody independent was really at risk. The SPE, its plant, and its debt then belong on the company's books.

Chewco

In 1993, Enron and the California Public Employees' Retirement System (CalPERS) formed an investment partnership called JEDI. Each contributed $250 million: Enron in its own stock, CalPERS in cash. Because the two shared control, Enron did not consolidate JEDI.{{A-031}}

In 1997, Enron needed a new partner to buy out CalPERS. The special committee found that Andrew Fastow, an Enron finance executive, first proposed to manage and invest in the new entity himself. It was called "Chewco Investments," named after the Star Wars character Chewbacca. After lawyers said Fastow's role would have to be disclosed in Enron's proxy statement, Michael Kopper, who worked for Fastow, took his place.{{A-032}}

On November 5, 1997, the board's Executive Committee approved Enron's guarantee of Chewco's loans by conference call. The special committee found that Fastow described Chewco as "an SPE not affiliated with either Enron or CalPERS," and that it saw no evidence that Kopper's role was disclosed to, or approved by, Lay or the board.{{A-033}}

The committee found that no outside investors could be found for Chewco. Instead, almost all of Chewco's $11.5 million of "equity" came from loans by the bank Barclays, documented so the bank could call them loans while Enron and Chewco called them equity. Kopper himself put in about $125,000.{{A-034}} The committee noted that financing SPEs this way was not unusual at the time.{{A-034@p. 50, II.D SPE Non-Consolidation "Equity" Requirement}} But the bank also required $6.6 million in cash to be set aside as collateral, and the committee concluded that "The existence of this cash collateral for the Barclays funding was fatal to Chewco's compliance with the 3% equity requirement."{{A-035}}

Kopper was paid about $2 million in fees relating to Chewco from 1997 to 2000.{{A-036}} When Enron bought out Chewco's interest in March 2001, the committee found, Kopper and another Chewco investor received about $10.5 million on their $125,000 investment.{{A-037}} Chapter 5 shows what it cost Enron when Chewco finally had to be brought back onto its books.

The LJM partnerships

On June 28, 1999, the board approved a partnership called LJM1 in which Fastow, by then Enron's chief financial officer (CFO), would be the general partner, the person who runs it. The board also ratified a finding that his role would not adversely affect Enron.{{A-040}} The Senate subcommittee staff later called this "an unprecedented arrangement allowing Enron's Chief Financial Officer to establish and operate the LJM private equity funds."{{B-072}}

In October 1999 the board approved Fastow's role in a second, larger partnership, LJM2. (The special committee and the Senate subcommittee differ by one day on the exact date.){{B-072}} The board attached controls: Enron's chief accounting officer, Richard Causey, and its chief risk officer, Richard Buy, were to approve every deal, and the Audit Committee was to review them each year. LJM2 attracted about 50 investors with $394 million in commitments.{{A-041}}

The document used to sell LJM2 to investors stressed Fastow's position. It said his "access to Enron's information pertaining to potential investments will contribute to superior returns."{{A-042}} This was a conflict of interest: as CFO, Fastow's job was to get the best deal for Enron, and as general partner, his job was to get the best deal for LJM.

From June 1999 through June 2001, Enron did more than 20 deals with the LJM partnerships. The special committee found that they increased Enron's reported results "by more than a billion dollars" while "enriching Fastow and his co-investors by tens of millions of dollars at Enron's expense."{{A-043}} In one pattern, Enron sold interests in seven assets to the LJM partnerships near the ends of reporting periods in 1999, then bought back five of them; the partnerships made a profit every time. The committee noted that some buybacks had plausible, more innocent explanations.{{A-044}}

Rhythms: a hedge with Enron's own stock

Chapter 2 introduced Enron's stake in Rhythms NetConnections. Enron invested $10 million in 1998. When Rhythms went public on April 7, 1999, its shares rose from $21 to $69 on the first day, and by May Enron's stake was worth about $300 million. But Enron was barred from selling before the end of 1999.{{A-045}} Because the stake was marked to market, a fall in Rhythms stock would cut Enron's reported profit.

The usual answer is a hedge: a contract with an outside party that pays you if your investment falls. On June 30, 1999, Enron built one with LJM1, funded mainly with Enron's own shares. A new entity, "Swap Sub," gave Enron a put option: the right to sell its Rhythms shares at $56 a share.{{A-046}}

The special committee explained the problem: "The normal idea of a hedge is to contract with a creditworthy outside party that is prepared--for a price--to take on the economic risk of an investment. If the value of the investment goes down, that outside party will bear the loss. That is not what happened here."{{A-047}} Swap Sub's ability to pay depended mainly on Enron's own stock. In early 2000, a team led by Enron's head of research, Vince Kaminski, estimated a 68% probability that the structure would default on what it owed Enron.{{A-049}}

When the Rhythms deal was unwound in 2000, Fastow and several Enron employees secretly invested in a partnership called Southampton Place. The special committee found that for a $25,000 investment, Fastow received, through a family foundation, $4.5 million in about two months, and two employees who each invested $5,800 received $1 million each.{{A-050}}

The Raptors

In 2000, Enron and LJM2 created four more SPEs, nicknamed the "Raptors," to offset losses on Enron's merchant investments. The committee found that they were not true hedges, because the Raptors' ability to pay rested mainly on Enron's own stock. "In effect," it wrote, "Enron was hedging risk with itself."{{A-051}} Kaminski later made the same point in sworn testimony to the bankruptcy examiner's lawyers, comparing it to buying house insurance from your own spouse: "if you go to your wife and buy insurance from her, there's a chance that the mortgage company will object."{{A-069}}

In the first Raptor, created in April 2000, LJM2 put in $30 million, while Enron put in about $537 million of its own stock and stock contracts.{{A-052}} The committee found that Andersen accountants were closely involved in structuring the Raptors.{{A-052@pp. 100-101, V.A.1 Raptor I: Formation and Structure}}

In the last two quarters of 2000, $532 million of Enron's pre-tax earnings came from the Raptors, more than 80% of the $650 million total.{{A-055}} From mid-2000 to late 2001, the committee calculated, Enron's pre-tax earnings would have been 72% lower without them.{{A-054}} For LJM2, the Raptors paid off quickly. Fastow reported to LJM2's investors in October 2000 rates of return of 193%, 278%, 2500%, and a projected 125% on the four Raptors.{{A-053}}

By December 2000, two Raptors lacked the money to pay what they owed Enron. On December 22, 2000, Enron pooled the four Raptors so it would not have to record a loss. The committee wrote: "We have seen no evidence that Enron's Board was informed of either the credit capacity problem or the solution selected to resolve that problem."{{A-056}} By March 2001 the shortfall had grown to $504 million. Rather than take a loss, Enron restructured the Raptors, in part by transferring to them more than $800 million of contracts to receive its own stock just before the quarter ended.{{A-057}} Employees told the committee that Skilling called fixing the Raptors a top priority. Skilling disputed this and said he recalled no significant involvement.{{A-057@pp. 121-122, V.D.2 The Restructuring Transaction}}

What it added up to

The bankruptcy examiner later summed up: "Although its SPE structures were complex, Enron's objectives were simple": to borrow money without recording debt, and to report the loan proceeds as cash from its operations.{{A-070}} The special committee found that Fastow was enriched by at least $30 million and Kopper by at least $10 million.{{A-060}}

As for the controls the board had relied on, the committee found that Causey and Buy interpreted their oversight roles very narrowly, and that Skilling "appears to have been almost entirely uninvolved in the process, notwithstanding representations made to the Board that he had undertaken a significant role."{{A-062}}

Enron's public filings did mention the LJM partnerships. But the committee found the disclosures "were obtuse, did not communicate the essence of the transactions completely or clearly, and failed to convey the substance of what was going on between Enron and the partnerships."{{A-064}} You can read one of those disclosures, phrase by phrase, on The Footnote page.

Ask Why

Enron's board approved Fastow's dual role and surrounded it with controls: two senior officers were to approve every deal, and a board committee was to review them each year. On paper, that sounds reasonable. Why might controls like these fail in practice, and what would you have required instead?
