Warning Signs and the Whistleblower

Well before the collapse, people inside Enron and its audit firm raised doubts. In August 2001, one employee took hers to the chairman.

Looking back, investigators found warning signs scattered across several years. Some were written down at the time. Others were described later, under oath. This chapter follows them roughly in order.

"Many push limits"

In February 1999, Arthur Andersen's lead partner on the Enron audit, David Duncan, prepared talking points for a meeting of Enron's Audit Committee, the group of board members that oversees the company's financial reporting.{{B-059}} According to the Senate subcommittee staff, his copy carried a handwritten note: "Obviously, we are on board with all of these, but many push limits and have a high 'others could have a different view' risk profile." The annotated document itself was not given to the committee during the meeting, but another Andersen partner confirmed through his lawyer that the handwriting was Duncan's and that the risk profile was discussed with the committee.{{B-063}}

What the directors took from such discussions is less clear. Robert Jaedicke, a former accounting professor, chaired the Audit Committee.{{B-077}} At a Senate hearing in May 2002, he was asked whether Andersen had told the committee that Enron used high-risk accounting. He testified: "We knew that the company was engaged in high-risk and innovative transactions."{{B-078}} But he said that, as far as he recalled, he never heard phrases such as "pushing the limits" or "form over substance" used.{{B-078@p. 32, Questioning by Senator Levin#42}}

Inside Enron, the risk analysts had their own doubts. As Chapter 3 described, the head of research, Vince Kaminski, told the special committee that in early 2000 his team had estimated a 68% chance that the Rhythms hedge structure would fail to pay what it owed Enron. (Causey said he did not recall that figure.){{A-049}}

Andersen weighs its client

On February 5, 2001, senior Andersen partners held a conference call to decide whether to keep Enron as a client. The firm rated Enron a "maximum" risk client. According to the bankruptcy examiner, an Andersen partner's e-mail the next day reported that "ultimately the conclusion was reached to retain Enron as a client."{{C-033}} The Senate Governmental Affairs Committee staff found that the same e-mail noted how "aggressive" Enron's accounting was.{{C-056}}

In early 2001, the examiner reported, Carl Bass, an accounting expert inside Andersen, was told that Enron's chief accounting officer, Richard Causey, had asked that Bass be removed from the Enron engagement, and that Andersen's senior executives had agreed.{{C-034}}

A sudden resignation

Jeffrey Skilling became Enron's chief executive in February 2001. Six months later, on August 14, 2001, he resigned.{{B-018}} The later federal indictment alleged that he left "with no forewarning to the public."{{B-017}} According to the SEC's later complaint, Kenneth Lay became chief executive again the same day.{{B-001}}

Skilling later told a Senate committee, under oath: "When I left Enron on August 14, I did not believe the company was in financial peril." He also testified that he had no knowledge of wrongdoing by Enron employees.{{B-023}}

Sherron Watkins

Sherron Watkins was an Enron vice president. She testified to the Senate Commerce Committee that she had been a certified public accountant since 1983 and had spent eight years at Arthur Andersen. Fastow hired her in 1993 to manage the JEDI partnership with CalPERS, and from mid- or late June to August 2001 she worked directly for him.{{B-054@pp. 10-11, Statement of Sherron Watkins#15}}

Watkins testified that on August 15, 2001, the day after Skilling resigned, she sent Lay an anonymous letter. She was responding to a request for questions for an all-employee meeting set for August 16.{{B-055}} She did not take her concerns to Skilling or Fastow. "To do so, I believed, would have been a job-terminating move," she testified.{{B-057}} (Her letter is quoted, as the Senate subcommittee staff reported it, on The Footnote page.)

On August 22, 2001, Watkins met Lay in person for just over half an hour. She used a memo titled "Summary of Raptor Oddities." She testified: "My main point to Mr. Lay was that by this time Raptor owed Enron in excess of $700 million under certain hedging agreements." Lay told her he would look into her concerns.{{B-056}} She testified that she had expected a thorough investigation and was disappointed that one did not happen.{{B-056}} Lay did not answer questions about this before Congress. As Chapter 7 describes, he declined to testify, invoking his Fifth Amendment right.{{B-008}}

Watkins appeared before Congress as a sworn witness. The library documents show no charges against her.{{B-058}}

An error comes to light

Watkins was not the only source of concern that summer. The special committee found that Enron and Andersen accountants realized in August 2001 that Enron had made an accounting error when it issued stock to the Raptors.{{A-059@p. 125, V.E Unwind of the Raptors}} (Chapter 5 explains the error.) According to a House subcommittee chairman's summary of what Duncan told congressional investigators, Andersen formed a consultation group on Enron in late August or early September 2001. Duncan said it responded to Watkins's allegations, to what the chairman's summary calls "the $1 billion accounting error discovered in August by Enron and Andersen with respect to the accounting for the Raptor entities," and to the falling value of assets in the Raptors.{{C-004}} This is a second-hand account: at the hearing, Duncan himself declined to answer questions.{{C-007}}

On September 26, 2001, Lay answered questions from employees in an online forum. The SEC later alleged that his statements there were false and misleading, including: "[t]he third quarter is looking great. We will hit our numbers."{{B-004}} According to the Justice Department's announcement of the charges, the indictment alleged that in the two months before that forum, Lay bought $4 million of Enron stock while selling $24 million of it through transactions that were not public.{{B-007}} These are allegations. Chapter 7 explains how Lay's case ended.

Meanwhile, the Raptors were being shut down. The special committee found that in mid-September 2001, Lay and Enron's chief operating officer, Greg Whalley, directed Causey to end them. Enron did so on September 28, 2001, paying LJM2 about $35 million.{{A-058}}

The watchdogs outside

Outside Enron, the SEC reviews companies' annual reports from time to time. The Senate Governmental Affairs Committee staff found that SEC staff had not reviewed any of Enron's annual reports after its 1997 report, partly because under the SEC's review cycle Enron was not due again until 2002.{{C-053}} The staff concluded that if the SEC had reviewed Enron's 2000 annual report, items such as "the now notorious footnote 16," on Enron's deals with related parties, were likely to have prompted questions.{{C-054}} That footnote is the subject of The Footnote page.

Ask Why

Watkins wrote anonymously, and she went to the chairman rather than to her own bosses, because she believed confronting them would cost her job. What makes it hard for people inside an organization to raise a concern, and what would have to be true for you to speak up?
