Arthur Andersen

Enron's auditor approved its books for years, then destroyed documents as investigators arrived. Its criminal conviction was later reversed, but by then the firm had collapsed.

Investors cannot check a company's books themselves. Public companies therefore hire an outside auditor, an independent accounting firm, to examine their financial statements and say whether they present the company's finances fairly. The auditor is paid by the company it checks. That is why its independence matters so much.

Enron's auditor

Arthur Andersen, which had audited InterNorth, became the auditor of the combined company formed when InterNorth acquired Houston Natural Gas in July 1985, which took the name Enron in April 1986.{{C-026}}{{A-001}} Its opinion on Enron's 2000 financial statements, dated February 23, 2001, said they "present fairly, in all material respects, the financial position of Enron Corp. and subsidiaries."{{A-027}} It was a big job. In February 2001, 113 Andersen professionals worked on the Enron engagement, 98 of them in Houston.{{C-023}}

From 1997 until December 2001, according to the SEC, Andersen's lead partner for the Enron audits was David Duncan.{{B-059}} He had taken over from a partner who had to step aside under Andersen's own rule that engagement partners rotate after seven years.{{C-036}}

The fees

Enron was one of Andersen's most important clients by fees, and those fees kept rising, the bankruptcy examiner reported.{{C-022}} Andersen earned about $50 million from Enron in 2000; sources give figures from $47.9 million to $54 million, depending on which accounting year and categories they use.{{C-021}} The Senate Governmental Affairs Committee staff found $52 million: $25 million for audit work and $27 million for consulting.{{C-019}} Andersen disputed the label. Its executive C.E. Andrews testified that "the audit-related fees on Enron were $25 million, essentially half of the total fee." Andersen partner Michael Odom added that a lot of the consulting was work typically done by the auditor.{{C-020}}

Some of that other work concerned the very deals in Chapter 3. The special committee found that Andersen billed Enron $5.7 million for advice on the LJM and Chewco transactions alone, "above and beyond its regular audit fees."{{A-061}} Across the profession, a former SEC commissioner told a House committee in 2002, non-audit services made up 73 percent of what audit clients paid their auditors on average in 2001.{{C-025}}

Errors and what the examiner concluded

According to the bankruptcy examiner, Andersen accountants later acknowledged three errors in the Enron audits. One was approving the "aggregation," or pooling, of the Raptors' credit. Another was letting Enron record the Raptor IOUs as assets, which overstated Enron's equity by $1 billion. The third was wrongly concluding that the Rhythms hedge entity met the 3% test.{{C-030}} Andersen's chief executive told Congress in December 2001, as quoted in the Powers Report: "When we reviewed this transaction again in October 2001, we determined that our team's initial judgment that the 3 percent test was met was in error."{{A-048}}

The examiner found evidence that "Enron officers withheld information from Andersen in numerous instances."{{C-029}} But he said that did not explain the whole story of Andersen's role. Going beyond it, he wrote, "the evidence suggests a concerted effort over a period of years by Andersen to assist one of its largest clients in taking advantage of rules-based GAAP accounting."{{C-027}} (GAAP, generally accepted accounting principles, is the set of rules U.S. companies must follow in their financial statements.) He concluded that the evidence would let a fact-finder (a judge or jury) decide that Andersen was negligent and helped Enron officers breach their duties to the company, while noting that Andersen would have defenses.{{C-028}} These are conclusions about what a court could find, not findings by a court.

The examiner also concluded that a fact-finder could find Andersen failed its duty to make sure Enron's Audit Committee understood how unusual transactions were accounted for.{{C-031}} From 1997 to November 2001, that committee met thirty times, usually with at least three Andersen partners present. The meetings generally lasted about one hour.{{C-035}}

The shredding

In the fall of 2001, as Enron's problems became public, Andersen instructed its employees to destroy documents under its document retention policy.{{C-042}} Congress investigated what happened. Much of the account below comes from a House hearing in January 2002, and the witnesses did not all agree.

Nancy Temple, a lawyer at Andersen, testified that she was first asked to join a call about an Enron accounting issue on September 28, 2001, and that between then and October 12 she gave legal advice, after consulting her supervisor and others, on documentation and retention issues.{{C-018}} On October 12 she e-mailed an Andersen partner: "It might be useful to consider reminding the engagement team of our documentation and retention policy."{{C-001}} Andersen's policy called for documents other than audit work papers to be discarded when no longer needed, but kept once a lawsuit started or was threatened. Andersen conceded that the policy was "not a model of clarity."{{C-017}}

On October 17, the SEC asked Enron for information about its accounting.{{C-012}} On October 23, according to Andersen's C.E. Andrews, Duncan "called an urgent meeting of the Enron engagement team, in which he organized an expedited effort to shred, or otherwise dispose of, Enron-related documents." Andrews testified that Duncan did this without consulting others in the firm or, so far as Andersen knew, its lawyers.{{C-011}}

Duncan's account, as a House subcommittee chairman summarized it from a staff interview, was different. Duncan said he had received Temple's October 12 e-mail, forwarded with the note "more help," and took it as legal advice to make sure his team followed the policy.{{C-002}} He said he had never before been asked about following the policy in his long career at Andersen.{{C-003}} By Duncan's account, the shredding continued until November 9, when Temple left him a voicemail ordering that all Enron documents be kept.{{C-006}}

Andersen said the destruction appeared to stop after Duncan's assistant e-mailed other secretaries on November 9, the day after Andersen received an SEC subpoena, telling them "no more shredding."{{C-013}} Andersen said a "very substantial volume" of documents had been destroyed, but that, so far as it could tell, no audit work papers were.{{C-014}}

Andersen reported the destruction to the Justice Department and the SEC on January 4, 2002, and disclosed it to the public on January 10.{{C-015}} It dismissed Duncan on January 15.{{C-016}} Two days later, Enron's board fired Andersen as its auditor.{{C-037}} On January 24, Duncan appeared before the House subcommittee and declined to answer questions, citing his constitutional right not to incriminate himself.{{C-007}} Declining to testify is a legal right and is not evidence of guilt.

Conviction, collapse, reversal

On March 7, 2002, a federal grand jury indicted Andersen for obstruction of justice.{{C-048}} After a trial in Houston, the firm was convicted on June 15, 2002.{{C-038}} Andersen told the SEC that it would "cease practicing before the Commission by Aug. 31, 2002," which meant it would no longer audit public companies.{{C-040}}

The firm did not survive as an operating business. The GAO, Congress's investigative and audit agency, reported that the indictment "led to a mass exodus of Andersen partners and staff as well as clients."{{C-047}} In 2001, Andersen had been the fourth-largest U.S. accounting firm, with global revenues over $9 billion; when indicted, it audited about 2,400 U.S. public companies.{{C-048}} According to published reports cited by the bankruptcy examiner, its headcount fell from 28,000 to about 150, plus a few dozen others, mainly lawyers.{{C-041}}

In 2005, the Supreme Court unanimously reversed Andersen's conviction and sent the case back to the lower court. According to the Syllabus (a summary prepared by the Court's Reporter of Decisions, not part of the opinion), the Court held that the jury instructions "failed to convey properly the elements" of the crime.{{C-043}} The jury had been told it could convict even if Andersen honestly believed its conduct was lawful. The Syllabus also notes that "Under ordinary circumstances, it is not wrongful for a manager to instruct his employees to comply with a valid document retention policy."{{C-044}} The reversal did not declare Andersen innocent; it found that the jury had been wrongly instructed. What happened in the case afterward is not covered by the library's documents.

Duncan's own case took longer. In 2008, the SEC alleged that he had been reckless in not knowing that the audit reports he signed on Enron's statements for 1998 through 2000 were materially false and misleading.{{B-060}} The SEC's Enron index describes that case as a "settled action"; its terms are not in the library.{{B-061}}

The Big Four

Andersen's fall reshaped the audit profession. The GAO reported that the number of firms able to audit large companies fell from eight in the 1980s to four, through mergers among six of the top eight and "the abrupt dissolution of Arthur Andersen LLP" in 2002. The remaining "Big 4" are Deloitte & Touche, Ernst & Young, KPMG, and PricewaterhouseCoopers.{{C-045}} By 2003 these four audited over 78 percent of U.S. public companies and 99 percent of public companies' annual sales.{{C-046}} Of 1,085 former Andersen public-company clients the GAO tracked, 938 moved to a Big 4 firm.{{C-049}} The GAO found no evidence yet that competition had suffered, but said the changes "may have implications for competition and public company choice."{{C-051}}

Ask Why

The Supreme Court reversed Andersen's conviction because the jury had been instructed incorrectly. By then the firm had already stopped auditing public companies and lost most of its people. What does Andersen's fate suggest about the difference between a legal verdict and the real-world consequences of being charged?
