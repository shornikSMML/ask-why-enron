Aftermath and Reform

Investigations, trials, lost retirement savings, and a new federal law that changed the rules for public companies and their auditors.

The investigations

Several bodies investigated what went wrong, and their conclusions carry different weight. Enron's own board formed a special committee in late October 2001, later chaired by William Powers Jr., dean of the University of Texas law school. Its lawyers reviewed more than 430,000 pages of documents and interviewed more than 65 people, but Fastow, Kopper, and Glisan declined to be interviewed on most issues, and the committee could not force anyone to testify.{{A-066}} Its report traced what happened to "failures at many levels and by many people," including "self-enrichment by employees," "inattentive oversight," and "a culture that appears to have encouraged pushing the limits."{{A-065}} Two of its three members concluded that "the Board of Directors failed, in our judgment, in its oversight duties."{{A-063}} Powers told a Senate committee in February 2002: "We found a systematic and pervasive attempt by Enron's management to misrepresent the company's financial condition."{{B-068}}

The Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations looked at the board. It found that the board "failed to safeguard Enron shareholders" and contributed to the collapse by allowing high-risk accounting, conflicts of interest, and undisclosed activity kept off the books.{{B-071}} Five directors, past and present, testified under oath in May 2002.{{B-073}}{{B-073@p. 2, Subcommittee Investigation#6}} All of them rejected any share of responsibility, and the subcommittee said its investigation "did not substantiate the claims that the Enron Board members challenged management and asked tough questions."{{B-074}} One director, John H. Duncan (no relation to Andersen's David Duncan), testified: "We worked hard. We prepared for meetings. We asked probing questions ..."{{B-075}} Another, Herbert Winokur Jr., testified that he saw Enron as "a cautionary reminder of the limits of a director's role."{{B-076}}

The subcommittee also found that the board failed to monitor Kenneth Lay's use of a company-financed personal credit line. It reported that from October 2000 to October 2001, Lay obtained over $77 million in cash this way and repaid it only with Enron stock.{{B-016@p. 50, Factual Basis (Excessive Compensation)#54}} Asked about it, the chairman of the board's compensation committee, Charles LeMaistre, testified: "We had never had any responsibility to monitor this."{{B-079!hrg-psi-board@p. 90, testimony of Charles LeMaistre#100}}

Finally, in the bankruptcy case, an examiner, Neal Batson, was appointed to investigate Enron's special purpose entities.{{A-095}} He concluded there was sufficient evidence for a fact-finder to conclude that Lay and Skilling breached their fiduciary duties, the legal duties officers and directors owe the company, by failing to respond to red flags about the misuse of SPEs, and that certain outside directors breached their duty of good faith by approving the Rhythms and certain Raptor hedges.{{A-097}} He also concluded that the evidence does not support a conclusion that the outside directors acted in bad faith in failing to respond to red flags.{{B-080}} These are civil conclusions about what a court could find, not criminal findings. The library documents show no charges against the outside directors.{{B-082}}

The trials

By July 2004, the Justice Department said eleven defendants had been convicted in its Enron cases.{{B-049}} How each case ended matters, and in several cases the library's documents tell only part of the story.

Michael Kopper, who ran Chewco, pleaded guilty on August 21, 2002 to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and money laundering and agreed to forfeit $4 million, according to the SEC.{{B-039}} Together with $8 million he agreed to pay in the SEC's civil case, that made $12 million, the total the Justice Department announced for "both this plea and a related SEC complaint."{{B-039}}{{G-013}} His sentence is still not in the library. In October 2002 the SEC charged Andrew Fastow, Enron's former chief financial officer, alleging that he ran a scheme to defraud Enron's investors and enrich himself.{{B-030}} The SEC's Enron index lists a January 2004 settlement of those civil charges.{{B-032}} The terms of that settlement are not in the library. The Justice Department announced that on January 14, 2004, Fastow pleaded guilty to two counts of conspiracy to commit securities and wire fraud and agreed to cooperate with the investigation.{{G-001}} Under his plea agreement, the department said, he would serve ten years in prison and forfeit more than $29 million.{{G-002}} On September 26, 2006, the department announced that he had been sentenced to six years in prison; that release said the plea agreement required him to forfeit more than $20 million.{{G-004}} The two releases give different forfeiture figures, and the library does not explain the difference, or why the sentence was shorter than the ten years first agreed. The former treasurer, Ben Glisan, pleaded guilty on September 10, 2003 to conspiracy to commit wire and securities fraud, and under his plea agreement was sentenced the same day to five years in prison, the Justice Department announced.{{G-006}} Both Fastow and Glisan later testified as government witnesses at the trial of Lay and Skilling.{{B-034}}

Richard Causey, the former chief accounting officer, pleaded guilty on December 28, 2005, three weeks before that trial was set to begin.{{B-044}} The Justice Department announced that on November 15, 2006 he was sentenced to 66 months in prison.{{G-009}} The SEC later settled its civil case against him and barred him from serving as an officer or director of a public company.{{B-045}}

Kenneth Lay appeared before a Senate committee in February 2002, was sworn in, and then declined to answer questions, invoking his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.{{B-008}} Invoking that right is not evidence of guilt. In July 2004 he was indicted on charges including securities fraud, wire fraud, and bank fraud.{{B-006}} In 2006 a jury convicted him of every count against him.{{B-010}} Lay died on July 5, 2006. According to the federal appeals court, his death caused the trial court to vacate his conviction and dismiss his indictment.{{B-011}} In law, he does not stand convicted.

Jeffrey Skilling was indicted in February 2004.{{B-021}} In May 2006 a jury found him guilty of nineteen counts, including conspiracy, securities fraud, and one count of insider trading, and not guilty of nine other insider-trading counts.{{B-025}} He was sentenced to 292 months in prison.{{B-026}} In 2009 a federal appeals court upheld his convictions but ordered a new sentence.{{B-027}} In 2010 the Supreme Court held that one legal theory used against him, "honest-services" fraud, covers only bribes and kickbacks, which the government never alleged he took. It affirmed the lower court in part, vacated it in part, and sent the case back.{{B-028}} The Court did not reverse his convictions outright. It left the lower court to decide whether the error was harmless and whether it affected his other convictions.{{B-028}} In April 2011 the appeals court held that the error was harmless, affirmed all of his convictions, and again ordered a new sentence.{{G-024}} According to a 2013 agreement between Skilling and the government, the Supreme Court declined to review that ruling in April 2012.{{G-026}} In that agreement, the two sides agreed to recommend a guidelines range of 168 to 210 months, and Skilling agreed to give up any further challenges to his convictions.{{G-027}} The sentence the court actually imposed after that agreement is not in the library.

Banks faced civil cases. In 2003, without admitting or denying the SEC's allegations, J.P. Morgan Chase agreed to pay $135 million and Citigroup $120 million to settle SEC charges. Of Citigroup's payment, $101 million related to its Enron dealings and the rest to another company, Dynegy. The charges concerned helping Enron disguise loans.{{B-086}} Arthur Andersen's conviction, as Chapter 6 described, was reversed in 2005.{{G-018}}

The employees' savings

Many Enron employees saved for retirement through a 401(k) plan. The GAO, Congress's investigative and audit agency, testified that Enron matched employees' contributions, but the match was invested only in Enron stock, and employees could not move it until age 50.{{C-061}} According to Labor Department figures cited by the GAO, 63 percent of the plan's assets were in company stock at the end of 2000.{{C-062}}

In the fall of 2001, as the stock fell, the plan went through a "lockdown," during which employees could not make changes, while it switched recordkeepers. The GAO called that an acceptable practice, but noted that executives faced no similar limits on company stock they held outside the plan.{{C-063}} Accounts of the lockdown's dates differ. One employee told the committee, in her written statement, that her notice said she could not access her account from October 20 to November 19, 2001.{{C-066}} A congressman described an "October 16 lockdown," and an employee of Enron's Oregon utility said workers there were effectively locked out in late September.{{C-065}}{{C-067}} The congressman told the committee that the plan lost "about $1 billion in value."{{C-065}} The Labor Department opened an investigation of Enron's pension plans on November 16, 2001.{{C-069}}

A new law

On July 30, 2002, President Bush signed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.{{C-070}} At the signing he said, "The auditors will be audited."{{C-071}} The law's main changes include:

  • A new overseer for auditors, the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), a nonprofit corporation that registers audit firms, sets auditing standards, and inspects firms.{{C-072}} Exactly two of its five members must be, or have been, certified public accountants.{{C-073}} The SEC declared it ready to operate on April 25, 2003.{{C-081}}
  • Limits on what auditors may sell the companies they audit, such as bookkeeping and many kinds of consulting. Other services, including tax work, need the audit committee's advance approval.{{C-074}}
  • Rotation of the lead audit partner after five years,{{C-075}} shorter than the seven years under Andersen's own policy.{{C-036}}
  • Personal certification: the chief executive and chief financial officer must certify each annual and quarterly report,{{C-076}} and a separate certification carries criminal penalties of up to 20 years in prison if willfully false.{{C-077}}
  • A yearly report by management on the company's internal controls over financial reporting, which the auditor must also check.{{C-078}}
  • A new crime of destroying records to obstruct a federal investigation, punishable by up to 20 years, and a requirement that auditors keep audit work papers for five years.{{C-079}}
  • Protection for employees who report suspected fraud against shareholders or violations of SEC rules to regulators, Congress, or a supervisor.{{C-080}}

Ask Why

Sarbanes-Oxley requires a company's chief executive and chief financial officer to personally certify every annual and quarterly report. Can a rule like that change how people behave, or does it mainly change who is held responsible when something goes wrong?
