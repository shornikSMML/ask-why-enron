In January 2003 the SEC told Congress it was not recommending further reforms to accounting and auditing, choosing instead to focus on putting the Act into effect and supporting the new Board.{{S-020}} Here is some of what followed.</p>  <h3>Section 404 in practice</h3> <p data-lens="auditors board">In June 2003 the SEC adopted rules for management's internal-control report, which must, among other things, name the control framework management used.{{T-007}} Management may not conclude that its controls are effective if there are one or more material weaknesses.{{T-009}} Before any company had complied, the SEC estimated the yearly cost of management's report at about $1.24 billion in total, a figure that left out the auditor's attestation.{{T-012}} The rule first set compliance for larger companies' fiscal years ending on or after June 15, 2004.{{T-008}} In February 2004 the SEC moved the dates to November 15, 2004 for larger companies, called <span class="term" data-term="accelerated-filer">accelerated filers</span>, and to July 15, 2005 for the rest.{{T-008!gao-06-361@p. 27, table of Section 404 compliance dates#32}} By spring 2006, GAO reported, the SEC had extended the smaller companies' deadline several more times.{{T-049}}</p>  <p data-lens="money">In April 2006 GAO found general agreement among regulators, companies, auditors and investors that the Act had a positive and significant effect on investor protection. It also found that smaller public companies bore "disproportionately higher costs (as a percentage of revenues)" in complying, especially with Section 404.{{T-046}} Many of them had documented their internal control for the first time.{{T-047}} An SEC advisory committee had drafted recommendations that, if adopted by the SEC, would exempt up to 70 percent of public companies from all or part of Section 404; in 2006 these were proposals, not law.{{T-049}}</p>  <p data-lens="auditors">In June 2007 the SEC issued guidance describing "a top-down, risk-based evaluation" of internal control,{{T-016}} and said management need not identify every control in a process.{{T-019}} On July 25, 2007 the SEC approved the PCAOB's Auditing Standard No. 5 for auditors' internal-control audits, replacing an earlier standard.{{T-022!gao-08-163@p. 14#19}} Its current version, AS 2201, says the auditor's objective is "to express an opinion on the effectiveness of the company's internal control over financial reporting,"{{T-023}} and that this audit "should be integrated with" the audit of the financial statements.{{T-022}}</p>  <p data-lens="auditors">In 2010 the Dodd-Frank Act added Section 404(c): the auditor's attestation "shall not apply" to a company that is neither a "large accelerated filer" nor an "accelerated filer."{{T-026}} Management's own report is still required. In GAO's 2006 account of the SEC's definitions, an accelerated filer had at least $75 million in public float, the value of shares held by outside investors; the definitions may have changed since.{{T-050}} A 2012 amendment also exempted "<span class="term" data-term="emerging-growth-company">emerging growth companies</span>" from the attestation.{{T-027}}</p>  <h3>The auditors and their overseer</h3> <p data-lens="auditors">The SEC's new auditor-independence rules took effect on May 6, 2003.{{T-029}} In 2010 the PCAOB itself was challenged in the Supreme Court. According to the Syllabus, a summary prepared by the Court's Reporter of Decisions rather than by the Court, the case began after the Board inspected an accounting firm, released a report critical of its auditing, and opened an investigation.{{T-039}} In <i>Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB</i>, decided June 28, 2010 by a vote of 5 to 4,{{T-040}} the Court held that "the dual for-cause limitations on the removal of Board members contravene the Constitution's separation of powers."{{T-041}} The opinion said this left Board members removable by the SEC at will, and that the Board's existence does not violate the separation of powers.{{T-042@561 U.S. at 508-509, Opinion of the Court, Part IV#32}} In the Syllabus's words, "the Board may continue to function as before."{{T-042}} According to the Syllabus, the Court also held that the SEC's appointment of Board members was valid.{{T-044}}</p>  <p data-lens="auditors">In July 2003 GAO reported that, after mergers and Andersen's dissolution, four firms audited over 78 percent of U.S. public companies (see <a href="chapters/ch6.html#big-four">Chapter 6</a>).{{S-023}} In January 2008 it found that the four largest firms still audited almost all large public companies, and that 82 percent of Fortune 1000 companies saw their choice of auditor as limited to three or fewer firms.{{T-052}} GAO saw no compelling need for immediate action, but warned that "the loss of another large firm would further reduce large companies' auditor choice."{{T-053}}</p>  <h3>Whistleblowers</h3> <p data-lens="knew">The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 also created SEC awards. When a whistleblower voluntarily gives the SEC original information that leads to a successful action with sanctions over $1 million, the SEC must pay an award of 10 to 30 percent, in total, of the money collected.{{T-054}} The same law protects such whistleblowers from retaliation; an employee who wins can get reinstatement and double back pay with interest.{{T-055}}</p>  <h3>What the library still doesn't cover</h3> <p>The library still has no PCAOB inspection reports on the largest firms, no PCAOB standards other than AS 2201, and nothing on later changes beyond the documents named here, so read this section as signposts rather than a full guide to today's rules.</p> |
